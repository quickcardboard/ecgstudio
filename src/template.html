    Using LiveHeart

    LiveHeart computes the ECG forward from the heart. A cellular automaton spreads activation through about 37,000 tissue cells. Each cell carries its own action potential. The 12 leads are calculated from the electrical field those cells produce, so every wave you see comes from the simulated tissue.

    Screen

    • Left: the heart. Pink tissue is resting, blue is depolarizing, green is repolarizing.
    • Right: a standard 12-lead layout (2.5 s per column) with a 10 s lead II rhythm strip, written by a sweeping cursor.
    • Drag the divider to resize the two sides, or press Expand on either side. Double-click the divider to return to 50/50.

    Controls

    • Sinus rate sets the sinus node rate (30–180 bpm). The rhythm stays sinus at every rate. Escape rhythms depend on where conduction is blocked, not on rate: a junctional escape (about 48 bpm) takes over when the sinus node or AV node is blocked, and a ventricular escape (40 bpm) when the block is below the AV node.
    • Time scale slows the whole simulation, down to 1/50 of real time, so you can follow the wavefront. The ECG slows with it.
    • View: External lets you rotate the heart; Front, Left, Inferior and Back jump to standard views. In Cutaway the heart can also be rotated: the cut plane always faces you, and Cut depth slides it toward or away from you. The presets jump to standard cuts. Anterior is the four-chamber cut. Lateral cuts the left ventricle from the patient's left, so the septum and the left bundle's fascicles face you. Short axis crosses both ventricles at mid level, seen from the apex, and is the best view for watching the delayed region in fascicular blocks. Cutaway shows the standard anterior four-chamber cut with the conduction system: SA node, AV node, His bundle, bundle branches and fascicles. The Purkinje network runs in the model but is not drawn.
    • Overlays: Labels names the chambers and vessels, and in the cutaway the conduction system too. Lead vectors draws the limb-lead hexaxial reference (amber, drawn from the torso's own lead vectors, so lead I is visibly shorter than II and III; dashed lines are each lead's negative half), the chest electrodes V1–V6 with their lead vectors (blue), and the live heart vector (white). When the heart vector points along a lead's arrow, that lead records a positive deflection.
    • Click on the heart to Stimulate: click atrial tissue for a premature atrial complex (PAC) or ventricular tissue for a premature ventricular complex (PVC), in either view. Tissue that is still refractory does not capture. Click twice quickly for a couplet.
    • Click on the heart to Block: in the cutaway, click any part of the conduction system and choose a block. The SA node offers SA exit block, a sinus pause or sinus arrest. The AV node offers 1st-degree, Mobitz I (Wenckebach), 2:1 and complete block. The His bundle offers Mobitz II and complete block. The bundle branches and fascicles can each be blocked. Blocked segments turn red, or orange for partial blocks, and are listed under Active blocks, where you can remove them. Escape rhythms appear only when conduction above them is lost.
    • Click on the heart for Ischemia: in the external view the main epicardial coronary arteries are drawn in red: the left main trunk dividing into the LAD and the circumflex, and the right coronary artery giving the posterior descending artery (a right-dominant heart). Each artery's territory includes the muscle fed by its smaller side branches. Click an artery to occlude it at that point. A yellow marker shows the occlusion, the vessel beyond it turns grey, and everything it supplies becomes transmurally ischemic (tinted purple, necrotic muscle grey). In the cutaway, click ventricular muscle on the cut face for subendocardial ischemia in a patch of that wall. Occlusions and patches are listed in the panel, where each can be removed.
    • Time since onset moves every ischemic region along the same time course. It stops only where the ECG changes character: onset, 15 min, 1 h, 4 h, 12 h, 2 days and 1 month. In the first minutes, K-ATP channels shorten the action potential: hyperacute T waves. Over the first half hour, depolarized ischemic cells drive an injury current: ST elevation over the territory with reciprocal depression opposite. Necrosis then advances from endocardium to epicardium over about six hours. Dead muscle loses its gap junctions and drops out of the QRS, so Q waves form. From about 12 hours the ST elevation eases and the T wave starts to invert. By 2 days the ST segment is back near baseline with deep symmetric T inversion. At 1 month the Q waves remain and the T waves are upright again. A proximal right coronary occlusion also starves the sinus-node and AV-node arteries, slowing the sinus rate and lengthening the PR; an occlusion beyond the crux slows only the AV node. A thin subendocardial patch gives ST depression rather than elevation.
    • Structure: RAE and LAE enlarge an atrium. The enlarged atrium carries more muscle, so its sources are stronger; LAE also delays conduction from the right atrium into the left, across Bachmann's bundle and the inferior connections, so the left atrium fires as a separate, later wave. RAE gives a tall, peaked P wave in II (P pulmonale). LAE gives a broad P wave of about 150 ms with two humps, right then left atrium, about 80 ms apart (P mitrale). LVH and RVH rebuild the heart with a thicker free wall: about 6 mm extra on the LV, 10 mm on the RV. Hypertrophied muscle carries more current, and the transmural repolarization gradient reverses ("strain"). LVH gives Sokolow–Lyon voltage with T inversion in I, aVL and V6; RVH a dominant R in V1, right axis deviation and T inversion in V1–V2. Building a configuration the first time takes a few seconds; after that it is kept, so switching back is immediate. Switching clears blocks and ischemia.
    • Accessory pathway (WPW): adds a fast, non-decremental muscle bridge across the AV ring at the chosen site (shown in blue). The ventricle near it is excited early, giving a short PR and a delta wave. The rest of the ventricle fuses with normally conducted activation, so the QRS is wide. The pathway also conducts backwards. Triggering AVRT: with a left lateral or left posterior pathway, click the left atrium for a PAC about 240–320 ms after the start of a P wave, which is during the QRS or early ST segment. The pathway is still refractory, so the PAC conducts down the AV node alone, with a narrow QRS. It then returns to the atria up the pathway and circles: orthodromic AVRT at about 190 bpm, with a retrograde P wave just after each QRS. Slowing time with the time scale makes the click easier. Reset ends it.
    • Pause freezes time. Reset restarts from rest and clears all blocks and ischemia.

    Banner

    The rhythm label comes from the simulator's own state, for example which pacemaker fired. The ischemia statements are read off the ECG the way a reader would: ST level 40 ms after the J point against the PR segment, new Q waves of at least 30 ms, and T waves compared with this heart's own healthy beat. They use the STEMI thresholds (0.1 mV in two contiguous leads, 0.15 mV in V2–V3). PR, QRS and QT are measured from tissue activation and repolarization times. QTc uses Bazett's formula. The axis is measured from the QRS areas in leads I and aVF.

    Model

    The anatomy is the average adult four-chamber heart of Rodero et al. (2021), built from CT scans and voxelized at 2 mm. The model includes fibre-dependent conduction, AV-node decremental conduction, restitution, and transmural and apicobasal repolarization gradients with electrotonic modulation. Electrical sources are stronger along muscle fibres than across them. Limb leads come from the lead fields of an insulated torso (a computed Burger triangle), and precordial leads blend the heart-vector projection with the near-field potential at each chest electrode. The averaged CT heart's LV free wall is thickened by about 6 mm and the heart sits slightly more vertical than in the scan; with these settings the model reproduces normal, left anterior and left posterior fascicular block axes.

    Anatomy: Rodero C, Strocchi M, et al. "Virtual cohort of extreme and average four-chamber heart meshes from statistical shape model." Zenodo, 2021, doi:10.5281/zenodo.4593739. Licensed CC BY 4.0. LiveHeart is an educational model, not a diagnostic device.
