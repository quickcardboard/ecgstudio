## 3. Learn text (the simulator’s Learn tab; Training opens it with “Show me why”)

Source: `TEACH` and `ISCH_TEACH` in `src/app.js`. Each entry shows its key features; the pitfalls of every entry on screen are listed together under “Pitfalls”.

### Sinus rhythm

- A P wave before every QRS, upright in I, II and aVF and inverted in aVR (the impulse starts high in the right atrium and spreads down and to the left).
- Constant PR interval, 120–200 ms.
- Rate 60–100 bpm (below 60 sinus bradycardia, above 100 sinus tachycardia); the name follows the P waves, not the rate.

Pitfalls:

- A sinus P is upright in II: if P is inverted in II, III and aVF the rhythm is not sinus (ectopic atrial or junctional).

### Atrial fibrillation

- No P waves: a wavy or flat baseline of fibrillatory (f) waves, often clearest in V1 and II.
- Irregularly irregular RR intervals with no repeating pattern.
- Narrow QRS unless there is bundle branch block or pre-excitation.

Pitfalls:

- A fast AF can look regular at a glance: measure several RR intervals.
- AF with WPW gives broad, bizarre, very fast beats (pre-excited AF): AV-nodal blockers are dangerous there.

### Atrial flutter

- Sawtooth flutter (F) waves, about 300/min, best seen in II, III, aVF (negative in typical counterclockwise flutter) and V1.
- Fixed AV conduction gives a regular rhythm: 2:1 (≈150 bpm), 3:1, 4:1; variable conduction gives an irregular one.

Pitfalls:

- 2:1 flutter at 150 bpm hides every other F wave in the QRS or T: suspect it whenever a regular narrow tachycardia runs at ~150.
- Adenosine slows the ventricular response and unmasks the F waves; it does not stop flutter.

### Ectopic atrial rhythm

- A P wave before each QRS but with an abnormal axis: here inverted in II, III and aVF (low right-atrial focus).
- PR often short (the focus lies close to the AV node); P shape constant, unlike MAT.

Pitfalls:

- Inverted inferior P waves with a short PR can also be junctional rhythm with retrograde P: in junctional rhythm the PR is usually < 120 ms or the P falls after the QRS.

### Wandering atrial pacemaker / multifocal atrial tachycardia

- At least three different P-wave shapes in one lead, each followed by a QRS.
- Varying PR and RR intervals: irregular. Rate below 100: wandering atrial pacemaker (WAP); above 100: multifocal atrial tachycardia (MAT). Set the rate with the slider.

Pitfalls:

- Easily mistaken for AF: look for a distinct P before every QRS. Classically seen in severe lung disease.

### AVNRT

- Regular narrow-complex tachycardia, typically 140–180 bpm, starting abruptly (often after a PAC with a long PR).
- No visible P before the QRS: the retrograde P is buried in the QRS or seen as a pseudo-r′ in V1 / pseudo-S in II.

Pitfalls:

- Adenosine (or vagal manoeuvres) terminates it, unlike atrial flutter or atrial tachycardia.

### Orthodromic AVRT

- Regular narrow-complex tachycardia, typically faster than AVNRT (often above 200 bpm); retrograde P after the QRS with RP > 70 ms (the impulse returns up the accessory pathway).
- In sinus rhythm the same patient may show pre-excitation (short PR, delta wave).

Pitfalls:

- A rate above 200 bpm and RP > 70 ms favour AVRT over typical AVNRT (140–180 bpm, the P hidden in the QRS).

### Torsades de pointes

- Polymorphic VT (often 200–250/min) whose QRS axis twists around the baseline: the complexes grow, shrink and change polarity in spindles every 5–20 beats.
- It arises on a long QT (congenital, drugs, low potassium or magnesium, bradycardia), typically after a short–long–short sequence of beats.
- Here: one ventricular focus that moves a step every beat around the left ventricle, between apex and base, so the activation direction turns through a full circle.

Pitfalls:

- Treatment differs from monomorphic VT: magnesium, raise the heart rate (pacing, isoprenaline), correct potassium, stop QT-prolonging drugs; defibrillate if it does not stop.
- Runs often stop on their own and recur; it can degenerate into VF.

### Ventricular tachycardia

- Regular broad-complex tachycardia (QRS ≥ 120 ms), rate > 100.
- AV dissociation: P waves march through at their own slower rate; capture and fusion beats prove it.
- Concordance (all chest leads positive or all negative) and extreme axis favour VT.

Pitfalls:

- A broad regular tachycardia is VT until proven otherwise, even if the patient looks well.

### Ventricular fibrillation

- Chaotic, irregular deflections of varying amplitude; no identifiable QRS, P or T waves.

Pitfalls:

- Check leads and the patient: artefact can mimic VF, but if pulseless, defibrillate now.

### 1st-degree AV block

- PR > 200 ms and constant; every P is followed by a QRS.

### Mobitz I (Wenckebach)

- PR lengthens beat by beat until a P is not conducted (dropped QRS); the cycle repeats.
- The longest PR is before the drop, the shortest just after; RR intervals shorten through the cycle (grouped beating).

Pitfalls:

- Usually AV-nodal and benign; compare with Mobitz II, where the PR does not change before the drop.

### 2:1 AV block

- Every second P is not conducted: two P waves for each QRS.

Pitfalls:

- With 2:1 block you cannot tell Mobitz I from II (there are no two consecutive conducted PRs). A narrow QRS favours the AV node; a broad QRS a lower block.

### Mobitz II

- Constant PR in conducted beats with sudden non-conducted P waves.
- Block below the AV node (His–Purkinje); often with a broad QRS.

Pitfalls:

- High risk of progression to complete heart block: pacing indication.

### 3rd-degree (complete) AV block

- P waves and QRS complexes are independent: regular P–P, regular R–R, PR varies at random.
- Escape rhythm: junctional (40–60 bpm, narrow QRS) or ventricular (20–40 bpm, broad QRS).

Pitfalls:

- Some P waves fall by chance at a "normal" PR: march out the P waves with calipers to prove dissociation.

### 2nd-degree SA exit block

- Dropped P–QRS–T complexes; the pause is an exact multiple of the P–P interval.

Pitfalls:

- Distinguish from sinus arrest, where the pause is not a multiple of the P–P interval.

### Sinus pause / arrest

- No P wave for a period that is not a multiple of the P–P interval; escape beats may appear (junctional: narrow, no preceding P).

### Escape rhythm

- Junctional: narrow QRS at 40–60 bpm with absent or retrograde P waves.
- Ventricular (idioventricular): broad QRS at 20–40 bpm.

Pitfalls:

- An escape rhythm is the heart rescuing itself: treat the cause, not the escape.

### Premature atrial contraction (PAC)

- An early P wave with a different shape, usually followed by a normal narrow QRS.
- Non-compensatory pause (the sinus node is reset).
- A very early PAC may not conduct at all (non-conducted PAC, P hidden in the T wave) or conduct with RBBB aberrancy.

Pitfalls:

- A non-conducted PAC hidden in the T wave looks like a sinus pause: look for a notch on the preceding T.

### Premature ventricular contraction (PVC)

- An early broad bizarre QRS without a preceding P, with the T wave opposite to the QRS.
- Usually a fully compensatory pause (the sinus node keeps its rhythm).

Pitfalls:

- A PVC on the T wave of an ischemic heart (R-on-T) can start VF.

### Right bundle branch block

- QRS ≥ 120 ms; rSR′ ("M") in V1–V2; wide slurred S in I, aVL, V5–V6.
- Secondary T inversion in V1–V3 is expected.
- The axis is unchanged (LV activation is normal): read it from the first 60 ms.

Pitfalls:

- A new RBBB with LAFB (bifascicular block) or with a long PR deserves attention.

### Left bundle branch block

- QRS ≥ 120 ms; broad notched R in I, aVL, V5–V6 without septal q; deep QS or rS in V1–V3.
- ST and T opposite to the main QRS deflection (appropriate discordance).

Pitfalls:

- LBBB hides ischemia and mimics anterior Q waves; use Sgarbossa criteria for infarction.

### Left anterior fascicular block

- Left axis deviation, −45° or beyond.
- qR in I and aVL; rS in II, III and aVF; QRS < 120 ms.

Pitfalls:

- Exclude other causes of left axis deviation (inferior MI, LVH) before calling LAFB.

### Left posterior fascicular block

- Right axis deviation (> +90°).
- rS in I and aVL; qR in II, III and aVF; QRS < 120 ms.

Pitfalls:

- A diagnosis of exclusion: rule out RVH, lateral MI and a vertical heart first.

### Left ventricular hypertrophy

- Voltage: Sokolow–Lyon S V1 + R V5/V6 ≥ 3.5 mV.
- Repolarisation abnormality ("strain"): down-sloping ST depression and asymmetric T inversion in I, aVL, V5–V6.

Pitfalls:

- Voltage alone is common in thin young adults; strain and left atrial enlargement add weight.

### Right ventricular hypertrophy

- Right axis deviation; dominant R in V1 (R/S > 1); deep S in V5–V6.
- Right ventricular strain: T inversion in V1–V3; often P pulmonale.

Pitfalls:

- Dominant R in V1 has other causes: posterior MI, RBBB, WPW (left-sided pathway), dextrocardia.

### Right atrial enlargement

- Tall peaked P ≥ 0.25 mV (2.5 mm) in II, III, aVF ("P pulmonale"); P duration normal.

### Left atrial enlargement

- Broad P ≥ 120 ms, notched in II with the humps ≥ 40 ms apart ("P mitrale").
- Deep, broad negative terminal P in V1 (≥ 1 mm × 1 mm).

### Wolff–Parkinson–White pattern

- Short PR (< 120 ms), delta wave (slurred QRS upstroke), QRS > 110 ms.
- Secondary ST/T changes opposite to the delta wave.
- Delta-wave polarity localises the pathway (e.g. negative delta in I/aVL: left lateral).

Pitfalls:

- Delta waves can mimic Q waves (pseudo-infarction) and hide in some leads: look in all twelve.

### Non-specific ST-T changes

- Flat T waves (between −1 and +1 mm) or minimal T inversion, with at most slight ST sagging, in leads where T is normally upright.
- They follow no coronary territory, and there is no ST depression of 1 mm or more and no Q waves.

Pitfalls:

- Common and usually benign: electrolytes (low potassium or magnesium), drugs (digoxin, antiarrhythmics), fast heart rate, hyperventilation, posture, or a meal. Report them as non-specific and compare with an earlier ECG; they are not a diagnosis of ischemia.
- Here: the gradient that makes the T wave upright (endocardium repolarising after epicardium) is reduced in patches across the ventricles.

### Acute pericarditis

- Diffuse concave ST elevation across many territories (I, II, aVL, aVF, V2–V6), with ST depression in aVR (and often V1).
- PR-segment depression (PR elevation in aVR) from the inflamed atria.
- No Q waves, no reciprocal depression outside aVR/V1; ST elevation often larger in II than in III. Sinus tachycardia is common.

Pitfalls:

- STEMI has territorial ST elevation with reciprocal depression and often convex ST: compare the distribution.
- Early repolarisation can look similar; the ST/T ratio in V6 > 0.25 favours pericarditis.

### STEMI in LBBB: Sgarbossa criteria

- LBBB normally has discordant ST-T: ST elevation where the QRS is negative (V1–V3) and depression where it is positive (I, aVL, V5–V6).
- Smith-modified Sgarbossa, any one positive: concordant ST elevation ≥ 1 mm in a lead with a positive QRS; concordant ST depression ≥ 1 mm in V1–V3; discordant ST elevation ≥ 1 mm and ≥ 25% of the depth of the preceding S wave.
- Here: the injury current adds to the block's secondary ST-T, so it shows where the two point the same way, or where the elevation is out of proportion to the QRS.

Pitfalls:

- A new or presumed-new LBBB alone is no longer treated as a STEMI equivalent: use the criteria and the clinical picture.
- Q waves and T inversion cannot be read for infarction in LBBB.

### Wellens syndrome

- T-wave changes in V2–V3 (often V1–V4) in a patient whose chest pain has settled: type A biphasic T (up, then down), type B deep symmetric T inversion.
- No Q waves, R waves preserved, ST at or near baseline (under 1 mm), normal or slightly raised troponin.
- Here: the anterior wall reperfused before it died; its action potentials stay long, most in the outer wall, so the T reverses over it.

Pitfalls:

- It marks a critical proximal LAD stenosis about to occlude: angiography, not a stress test.
- Type A evolves into type B; both can be missed because the patient is pain-free when the ECG is taken.

### Hyperkalemia

- Tall, narrow-based, peaked ("tented") T waves, symmetric, best seen in V2–V4 and II: the earliest sign (K about 6–7 mmol/L).
- Higher: the P wave flattens and widens, the PR lengthens, and the QRS widens; at the extreme the QRS merges with the T into a sine wave.
- Here: faster repolarisation (more potassium current) makes every cell repolarise steeply and the T narrow; at higher levels conduction slows in the atria and ventricles.

Pitfalls:

- The ECG can be normal even at dangerous potassium levels: treat the number, not only the ECG.
- Tall T waves of early repolarisation and hyperacute T waves of ischemia are broader-based; the hyperkalemic T is narrow and pointed.

### Hypokalemia

- Flattened T waves with prominent U waves (a second hump after the T), best seen in V2–V3; the U can exceed the T.
- Slight ST depression; the T and U merge into a long "QU" interval.
- Here: less potassium current slows the last part of repolarisation: a share of it is left to a slow tail, which writes the U wave.

Pitfalls:

- Measure the QT to the end of the T, not the U: hypokalemia mimics long QT.
- A U wave is normal in V2–V3 at slow heart rates if small (under ~1.5 mm and much smaller than the T).

### Hypocalcemia

- Long QTc from a long, flat ST segment; the T wave itself keeps its normal shape and width.
- Here: a longer plateau (phase 2) of the action potential, so repolarisation starts later everywhere.

Pitfalls:

- Distinguish from congenital long QT and drugs, which mostly broaden or change the T wave itself.

### Hypercalcemia

- Short QTc with a short or absent ST segment: the T wave starts almost right after the QRS.
- Here: a shorter plateau (phase 2) of the action potential.

Pitfalls:

- Severe hypercalcemia can show J waves (like hypothermia) and bradyarrhythmias.

### Hypothermia

- J (Osborn) waves: a positive hump at the junction of the QRS and ST, best seen in the inferior and lateral leads; it grows as the temperature falls.
- Sinus bradycardia, long PR, long QT, slightly wide QRS; shivering artefact is common; atrial fibrillation and ventricular arrhythmias at lower temperatures.
- Here: a notch-and-dome of the epicardial action potential (transient outward current), a longer action potential and slower conduction.

Pitfalls:

- J waves also appear in hypercalcemia, brain injury and early repolarisation; the clinical context decides.

### Brugada syndrome (type 1)

- Coved ST elevation ≥ 2 mm (J point) in V1–V2, descending into a negative T wave.
- Often an rSR′ or a terminal r′ in V1; limb leads usually normal.
- Only type 1 is diagnostic; higher V1–V2 placement (2nd–3rd intercostal space) can unmask it.

Pitfalls:

- Fever and sodium-channel blockers accentuate the pattern; RBBB, pectus excavatum and high-K can mimic it.

### Adenosine

- Transient AV-nodal block for seconds: P or flutter waves continue without QRS complexes.

### ST-elevation myocardial infarction

- ST elevation ≥ 1 mm in two contiguous leads (≥ 1.5–2.5 mm in V2–V3, depending on age and sex), measured at the J point.
- Reciprocal ST depression in the opposite leads supports a true infarction.
- Territory: V1–V4 anterior (LAD; add I, aVL for proximal); II, III, aVF inferior (RCA or LCx; ST elevation III > II suggests RCA); I, aVL, V5–V6 lateral (LCx, diagonals); ST depression V1–V3 posterior.

Pitfalls:

- Pericarditis gives diffuse concave ST elevation without reciprocal depression (except aVR) and with PR depression.
- With inferior STEMI look at V1 (and right-sided leads) for right ventricular involvement.

### Hyperacute phase

- The first minutes: tall, broad, symmetric T waves over the territory before ST elevation develops.
- Judge the T against the QRS in front of it: a T whose area is large for a small QRS, in two contiguous leads, is more telling than height alone.
- Symmetric: the T falls about as slowly as it rises, on a broad base (a normal T rises slowly and falls faster).

Pitfalls:

- Compare with the patient’s baseline: hyperacute T waves are easy to miss on a single ECG.
- Peaked but narrow-based T waves suggest hyperkalemia rather than ischemia.

### Evolving infarct

- Q waves (≥ 40 ms or ≥ 1 mm deep in two contiguous leads) or QS complexes appear within hours.
- ST elevation resolves over days while T waves invert.
- Lost R waves (poor R-wave progression) are the precordial equivalent of Q waves.

Pitfalls:

- Persistent ST elevation weeks after an infarct suggests a ventricular aneurysm.

### Old (healed) infarct

- Q waves remain; ST is at baseline; T inversion often persists for months but may normalise.

Pitfalls:

- Q waves alone cannot date an infarct.

### Subendocardial ischemia (NSTEMI pattern)

- Horizontal or down-sloping ST depression, often widest in V4–V6 and II, with ST elevation in aVR.
- T waves may flatten or invert; no Q waves; the ECG may normalise after the episode.

Pitfalls:

- Caused by a narrowed artery or by demand outstripping supply: flow is reduced, not stopped, so the inner wall (farthest from the epicardial arteries, under the highest wall stress) suffers first. A complete occlusion makes the whole wall ischemic instead: ST elevation.
- ST depression does not localise the ischemic region well; ST elevation in aVR with widespread depression suggests left main or three-vessel disease.
