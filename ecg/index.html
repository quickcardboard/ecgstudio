Rhythms and tachycardias

  • Normal sinus rhythmNormal sinus rhythm is the heart's normal rhythm: each beat starts in the sinus node, every P wave is followed by a QRS complex, and the rate is 60 to 100 beats per minute.
  • Atrial fibrillationAtrial fibrillation (AF) is chaotic electrical activity in the atria: there are no P waves, the baseline shows fine fibrillatory waves, and the QRS complexes come at irregularly irregular intervals.
  • Atrial flutterTypical atrial flutter is a macro-reentrant circuit around the right atrium that produces regular sawtooth flutter waves at about 300 per minute, usually conducted to the ventricles 2:1 at about 150 bpm.
  • Multifocal atrial tachycardiaMultifocal atrial tachycardia (MAT) is an irregular rhythm over 100 bpm in which at least three different P-wave shapes come from several atrial foci, each with its own PR interval.
  • AV nodal re-entrant tachycardia (AVNRT)AV nodal re-entrant tachycardia (AVNRT) is the commonest regular narrow-complex supraventricular tachycardia: a re-entry circuit inside the AV node, using a slow and a fast pathway, drives the ventricles at about 140–250 bpm.
  • AV re-entrant tachycardia (AVRT)AV re-entrant tachycardia (AVRT) is a supraventricular tachycardia in which the impulse loops between atria and ventricles through the AV node and an accessory pathway.
  • Ventricular tachycardiaVentricular tachycardia (VT) is a wide-complex tachycardia that starts in the ventricles: three or more consecutive ventricular beats over 100 bpm, often with AV dissociation.
  • Torsades de pointesTorsades de pointes is a polymorphic ventricular tachycardia in which the QRS axis twists around the baseline, with complexes waxing and waning in height.
  • Ventricular fibrillationVentricular fibrillation (VF) is disorganised electrical activity of the ventricles with no effective contraction.

Conduction blocks

  • First-degree AV blockFirst-degree AV block is a fixed delay in AV conduction: the PR interval is longer than 200 ms, but every P wave is still followed by a QRS complex.
  • Second-degree AV block, Mobitz I (Wenckebach)Mobitz type I (Wenckebach) second-degree AV block is a progressive lengthening of the PR interval from beat to beat until one P wave is not conducted, after which the cycle starts again.
  • Second-degree AV block, Mobitz IIMobitz type II second-degree AV block is the sudden failure of a P wave to conduct with no warning: the PR interval stays the same before and after the dropped beat.
  • 2:1 AV blockIn 2:1 AV block every second P wave is not conducted, so there are two P waves for every QRS complex.
  • Third-degree (complete) heart blockIn complete (third-degree) heart block no atrial impulse reaches the ventricles.
  • Left bundle branch blockIn left bundle branch block (LBBB) the left ventricle is activated late, from the right side across the septum.
  • Right bundle branch blockIn right bundle branch block (RBBB) the right ventricle is activated late, so the end of the QRS points to the right and forward.
  • Left anterior fascicular blockLeft anterior fascicular block (left anterior hemiblock) is the commonest cause of marked left axis deviation: the QRS axis lies between −45° and −90°, with qR complexes in I and aVL and rS complexes in II, III and aVF, and a QRS under 120 ms.
  • Left posterior fascicular blockLeft posterior fascicular block (left posterior hemiblock) shifts the QRS axis to the right, beyond +90°, with rS complexes in I and aVL and qR complexes in III and aVF.
  • Sinoatrial exit blockIn sinoatrial (SA) exit block the sinus node keeps firing but some impulses fail to leave it, so a whole P–QRS–T complex is missing.

Ischemia and infarction

  • Anterior STEMI (LAD occlusion)An anterior ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) is caused by occlusion of the left anterior descending (LAD) artery.
  • Inferior STEMI (RCA occlusion)An inferior ST-elevation myocardial infarction shows ST elevation in leads II, III and aVF, usually with reciprocal ST depression in I and aVL.
  • Lateral STEMI (circumflex occlusion)A lateral myocardial infarction, usually from occlusion of the left circumflex (LCx) artery or a diagonal branch of the LAD, shows ST elevation in the lateral leads I, aVL, V5 and V6.
  • Hyperacute T wavesHyperacute T waves are tall, broad-based, symmetric T waves over the territory of a freshly occluded coronary artery.
  • Old inferior myocardial infarctionAn old (healed) myocardial infarction is recognised on the ECG by pathological Q waves in the leads over the scar, here II, III and aVF after an inferior infarct, with the ST segment back at baseline and T-wave inversion that may persist for months.
  • Wellens syndromeWellens syndrome is a pattern of deep, symmetric (type B) or biphasic (type A) T waves in V2–V3 in a patient whose chest pain has settled.

Chambers, pre-excitation and other conditions

  • Left ventricular hypertrophyLeft ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) raises the QRS voltages that face the left ventricle: tall R waves in the lateral leads and deep S waves in V1–V2.
  • Right ventricular hypertrophyRight ventricular hypertrophy (RVH) shifts the QRS forces to the right and forward: right axis deviation, a dominant R wave in V1, deep S waves in V5–V6, and often right atrial enlargement and right ventricular strain.
  • Wolff–Parkinson–White patternIn Wolff–Parkinson–White (WPW) pattern an accessory pathway lets the atrial impulse reach the ventricles early, bypassing the AV node.
  • Acute pericarditisAcute pericarditis causes widespread concave ST elevation in most leads, PR-segment depression, and ST depression with PR elevation in aVR, without the reciprocal changes or Q waves of a myocardial infarction.
  • Brugada syndrome (type 1)Brugada syndrome is an inherited sodium-channel disorder with a risk of ventricular fibrillation and sudden death.
  • Right atrial enlargementRight atrial enlargement makes the P wave taller but not longer: a peaked P wave over 2.5 mm in lead II (P pulmonale) and a prominent initial positive P wave in V1.
  • Left atrial enlargementLeft atrial enlargement makes the P wave longer: a broad, notched P wave of 120 ms or more in lead II (P mitrale) and a deep, wide terminal negative part of the P wave in V1.

Electrolytes and temperature

  • HyperkalemiaHyperkalemia (high serum potassium) first causes tall, narrow, peaked T waves; as potassium rises further the P waves flatten and disappear, the PR and QRS lengthen, and the ECG can progress to a sine-wave pattern, ventricular fibrillation or asystole.
  • HypokalemiaHypokalemia (low serum potassium) flattens the T waves, brings out prominent U waves, depresses the ST segment, and prolongs the apparent QT (in fact the QU) interval, raising the risk of arrhythmias including torsades de pointes.
  • HypocalcemiaHypocalcemia (low serum calcium) prolongs the QT interval by lengthening the ST segment, while the T wave itself keeps a normal shape and width.
  • HypercalcemiaHypercalcemia (high serum calcium) shortens the QT interval by shortening the ST segment, so the T wave seems to start right after the QRS.
  • HypothermiaHypothermia slows the heart and every interval and produces the characteristic Osborn wave: a positive hump at the J point, at the junction of the QRS and ST segment, most visible in the inferior and lateral precordial leads.
